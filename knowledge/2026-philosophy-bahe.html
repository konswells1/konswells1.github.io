BAHE Research Philosophy

BAHE Research Philosophy

BAHE research is shaped not only by the questions we ask and the methods we use, but also by how we reason, collaborate, learn and take responsibility for the knowledge we produce. This philosophy makes those commitments explicit and provides a shared framework for research practice.

Why this philosophy matters

Many important research expectations are communicated informally: how to recognise a worthwhile question, how cautiously to interpret evidence, how to prepare for discussion, how to share responsibility, and how to support another researcher without taking ownership of their work.

Making these expectations visible helps BAHE members and collaborators understand the basis of our research practice. It also creates a foundation for more specific guidance, including Planning and Communicating a Degree Research Project, reproducible-workflow guidance and collaboration resources.

Our mission

BAHE seeks to develop rigorous, useful and trustworthy scientific knowledge while supporting people to grow as capable, independent and responsible members of the scientific community.

These aims are inseparable. Excellent research depends on people who are able to think critically, contribute reliably, communicate openly and work with integrity. Researcher development, scientific output and research culture are therefore treated as connected responsibilities rather than separate goals.

Our core values

Scientific integrity

We seek conclusions that are justified by evidence, transparent about uncertainty and open to correction.

Shared responsibility

Every member of the research community contributes according to their role, expertise, opportunities and agreed commitments. Responsibilities differ, but they remain reciprocal and connected to a common scientific mission.

Accountability means communicating progress and difficulty honestly, documenting consequential decisions, fulfilling agreed contributions, and being prepared to explain how one’s actions affect the work of others.

Shared responsibility does not guarantee identical outcomes. Projects differ in opportunity, difficulty, resources and results. Accountability concerns whether agreed contributions were pursued rigorously, communicated honestly and adapted responsibly—not whether every project produced the same number or prestige of outputs.

Intellectual development

Research should help people develop judgement, confidence, capability and increasing independence while recognising when advice, collaboration or specialist expertise is needed.

Fairness and recognition

Opportunities, support, workload, decision-making and scientific credit should be discussed openly and allocated fairly. Contributions should be recognised according to their substance rather than hierarchy or visibility.

Inclusion and equitable participation

BAHE values different backgrounds, perspectives, capabilities and ways of thinking. People should have equitable opportunities to participate, develop and contribute, with reasonable flexibility for disability, health, caring responsibilities, part-time work, remote participation and other circumstances.

Equity does not require identical arrangements or expectations. It requires transparent decisions, appropriate support and fair opportunities to contribute to the common scientific mission.

Kindness with ambition

Respect, friendliness and enjoyment are important parts of scientific life. They should coexist with honest feedback, difficult questions, high standards and sustained effort. Kindness means investing in one another’s development, not avoiding necessary challenge.

Scientific ambition should be pursued through sustained and well-organised effort, not through the normalisation of avoidable crisis, chronic overwork or unclear boundaries. Periods of exceptional intensity may occur, but they should not replace realistic planning, appropriate resourcing and respect for health and personal circumstances.

Environmental responsibility

Research design should consider environmental and societal responsibilities, including travel, sampling, disturbance, material use and computing, and should reduce avoidable impacts without compromising scientific validity or essential collaboration.

NoteBAHE Reflection — Kindness and Excellence

Scientific excellence and human kindness should reinforce one another.

Respect, friendliness, encouragement and enjoyment are important parts of a healthy research culture. They help create an environment in which people feel able to ask questions, discuss ideas openly, admit uncertainty and learn from mistakes.

However, kindness is not measured simply by being agreeable.

Sometimes the kindest action is investing time to provide careful feedback, asking difficult scientific questions, challenging weak arguments or helping a researcher recognise problems before they become larger ones.

Likewise, scientific excellence should never become an excuse for neglecting people. Researchers deserve clear expectations, honest communication, fair opportunities, appropriate mentorship and respect for their individual circumstances.

Within BAHE, we therefore view kindness not as lowering standards, but as helping one another achieve standards that would be difficult to reach alone.

The strongest research communities are those in which people challenge ideas rigorously while supporting one another generously.

What BAHE expects

BAHE members and collaborators are expected to:

  • communicate progress, uncertainty and difficulty honestly;
  • prepare for scientific discussions and act thoughtfully on advice;
  • document important decisions and maintain reliable research records;
  • fulfil agreed contributions or raise problems early when this is not possible;
  • recognise the intellectual and practical contributions of others fairly;
  • contribute to a supportive, inclusive and ambitious research culture; and
  • take responsibility for how their work affects collaborators, resources and the wider scientific mission.

These expectations apply across career stages and roles. They do not imply that everyone has identical duties. Responsibility should remain proportionate to experience, authority, opportunity and agreed commitments.

TipHow responsibility is shared

Shared responsibility does not mean identical responsibility. Each person is accountable for contributions appropriate to their role, authority, expertise, opportunities and agreed commitments.

Students, researchers, technicians, supervisors, Principal Investigators and collaborators may therefore carry different duties, but all are expected to communicate honestly, contribute reliably, document important decisions and consider how their work affects others.

Senior roles carry additional responsibilities for leadership, resources, fairness, researcher development and the integrity of the wider programme.

How these values guide our research practice

Develop worthwhile and answerable questions

Research should begin with questions that are scientifically important, clearly framed and suitable for the evidence that can realistically be obtained.

Methods are chosen in response to the question. Technical novelty, available data or familiar analytical tools may create opportunities, but they should not determine the research purpose by themselves.

In practice, this means:

  • explaining why the question matters;
  • distinguishing broad motivation from a testable research question;
  • separating exploratory aims from confirmatory tests;
  • considering whether the proposed evidence can address the intended claim; and
  • revising the question when the available design cannot support it.

Match claims to evidence

Scientific conclusions should remain proportionate to the study design, data, analysis and uncertainty.

Strong research does not remove uncertainty by assertion. It identifies what is supported, what remains ambiguous and which alternative explanations are still plausible.

In practice, this means:

  • distinguishing observations, results, interpretations and implications;
  • reporting uncertainty rather than presenting estimates as exact;
  • avoiding causal language when the design supports only association;
  • considering measurement, sampling and model limitations; and
  • treating limitations as part of interpretation rather than as a final disclaimer.

Build reproducibility into research

Reproducibility is a feature of the research process, not an administrative task added at the end.

Data, code, decisions and documentation should be organised so that the work can be understood, checked and extended by the original researcher and, where appropriate, by others.

In practice, this means:

  • preserving raw data and documenting all transformations;
  • using clear and stable file structures;
  • recording analytical decisions and deviations from initial plans;
  • applying version control where it improves traceability;
  • generating results and figures through reproducible workflows; and
  • sharing data, code and metadata when ethical, legal and contractual conditions permit.

Treat collaboration as a scientific practice

Collaboration is not merely the division of tasks. It is a research practice requiring clarity, fairness, accountability and respect.

Expectations can change as projects develop, so roles and responsibilities should be discussed more than once rather than assumed at the beginning.

In practice, this means:

  • clarifying aims, roles, responsibilities and decision processes;
  • discussing authorship and contributorship early and revisiting them later;
  • documenting consequential decisions;
  • communicating delays, uncertainty and disagreement directly;
  • recognising intellectual, practical and relational contributions fairly; and
  • approaching criticism as an effort to improve the work rather than to establish status.

Scientific disagreement should be addressed directly, respectfully and with reference to evidence. When concerns about conduct, workload, authorship, supervision or research integrity cannot be resolved informally, members should be able to use appropriate group, institutional or independent routes for advice and escalation without disadvantage.

Shared responsibility does not create an unlimited obligation for one person to complete another person’s unfinished contribution. Additional investment in analysis, writing or project recovery should be considered in relation to the scientific value of the work, prior contributions, agreed roles, available capacity and fairness across the wider research programme.

Those with greater authority have a corresponding responsibility to use it carefully. Leadership should create clarity, protect research integrity, distribute opportunities fairly, recognise dependence and vulnerability, and make it possible for concerns or disagreement to be raised without retaliation.

Develop researchers and scientific judgement

Research should contribute not only to new knowledge, but also to the development of people.

BAHE aims to help researchers progressively develop scientific judgement, critical thinking, confidence, independence, collaboration, communication and leadership appropriate to their career stage.

Support should respond to experience and need. Providing structure is often necessary, but repeatedly replacing another researcher’s reasoning with ready-made answers limits long-term development. Equally, independence should never become an excuse for withholding guidance when it is genuinely needed.

In practice, this means:

  • preparing thoughtfully for scientific discussions;
  • identifying decisions and uncertainties clearly;
  • bringing attempted solutions as well as problems;
  • making reasoning explicit rather than simply seeking approval;
  • using feedback to strengthen judgement rather than replace it;
  • supporting the development of others as experience grows; and
  • progressively taking greater responsibility for scientific decisions and leadership.

Practise responsible openness

BAHE aims to make research and knowledge as open as possible while respecting ethical, legal, ecological and contractual responsibilities.

Openness includes transparency about uncertainty, limitations, authorship, provenance and the conditions under which knowledge was produced. Responsibility also requires reflection on who may benefit, who may bear risks and how research materials could be reused.

In practice, this means:

  • planning appropriate routes for sharing outputs;
  • protecting sensitive people, species, sites and data;
  • providing clear attribution and provenance;
  • recognising the limits of generalisation;
  • correcting errors openly; and
  • reviewing established practices when better approaches become available.

Steward scientific contributions

Scientific ideas, data, analyses and publications are often developed through collective effort.

BAHE values transparent discussion of intellectual contributions, contributorship, authorship, data stewardship, confidentiality and publication planning.

Recognition should reflect substantive scientific contributions and accepted disciplinary standards. Temporary confidentiality may sometimes be necessary to protect collaborators, students, sensitive information or planned scientific outputs, but should always be proportionate, justified and consistent with the principles of scientific integrity and responsible openness.

From philosophy to practice

The principles above are intentionally broad. More specific BAHE knowledge objects translate them into practical guidance for recurring research situations.

Meetings and supervision

Meetings should be prepared, purposeful and focused on questions, decisions or feedback that benefit from discussion. The related Planning and Communicating a Degree Research Project guide provides practical guidance for preparing, documenting and using supervision effectively.

Feedback

Feedback should be specific, proportionate and directed toward improving both the work and the researcher’s capacity to evaluate it independently.

Collaboration

Project roles, communication routes, decision processes and contributorship should be made explicit and revisited as the project evolves.

Open and reproducible research

Data stewardship, analysis, documentation and dissemination should be planned together, with openness constrained only where justified by ethical, legal or practical responsibilities.

A shared measure of excellence

Research groups and individuals should be judged not only by the papers they publish, but also by the researchers they help develop, the responsibilities they fulfil and the scientific communities they help build.

Excellence depends on sustained effort, but effort alone is not enough. Responsibility, opportunity, support, fair recognition and scientific ambition must develop together.

Scope and review

This philosophy provides direction rather than inflexible rules. Its application should account for disciplinary context, career stage, project scale, available resources, ethical obligations and the needs of research partners.

The document should be reviewed and revised when BAHE’s collective experience identifies important omissions, ambiguities or better ways of working. The philosophy should remain open to constructive discussion by BAHE members and the wider research community, while retaining its commitments to diversity, open science, scientific integrity and rigorous research practice.
