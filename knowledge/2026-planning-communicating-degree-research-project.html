Planning and Communicating a Degree Research Project

Planning and Communicating a Degree Research Project

A degree research project is both a learning process and a contribution to scientific knowledge. The balance between these purposes changes from BSc and taught MSc dissertations to MRes and PhD research, as supervision develops from structured guidance towards scientific advice, collaboration and shared responsibility for progress and outputs.

Why this guide is needed

A degree research project is not the same experience at every stage. A BSc or taught MSc dissertation is usually designed primarily to help a student learn how research questions are developed, investigated and communicated. An MRes or PhD project is also a training experience, but it is more often embedded within a wider research programme involving collaborators, shared datasets, funded work, institutional commitments or planned scientific outputs.

These differences affect how projects should be planned, how supervision works and how progress should be communicated. They do not imply that one stage is more valuable than another. They reflect different educational purposes and different levels of responsibility.

This guide provides a common framework while recognising that programme regulations, scholarship conditions, project grants and individual supervisory agreements may create additional formal requirements. Those formal conditions should always be read directly and discussed explicitly rather than inferred from general guidance.

NoteHow to use this guide

This article provides BAHE guidance on research planning, supervision and communication. It does not replace formal degree regulations, scholarship or studentship conditions, employment contracts, grant requirements, ethical approvals or individual supervisory agreements. Where formal requirements apply, they take precedence and should be discussed explicitly.

A degree project has two connected purposes

Every degree research project combines:

  1. development of the researcher through reading, planning, analysis, discussion and reflection; and
  2. development of the research through evidence, interpretation, documentation and outputs.

The balance changes with the degree.

For many BSc and taught MSc projects, the educational purpose is dominant. The project may be relatively self-contained, and its main value may lie in helping the student understand the research process and demonstrate scientific judgement.

For many MRes and PhD projects, learning and contribution are more tightly coupled. The researcher may work within a grant, laboratory, field programme, collaboration or long-term dataset. Decisions and delays may affect other people, planned outputs or external commitments. The project therefore involves greater responsibility for communication, documentation and sustained progress.

How supervision changes across stages

Supervision should develop with the researcher. It is not simply reduced as a student becomes more advanced; its purpose changes.

BSc dissertation

The supervisor commonly acts primarily as a teacher, adviser and mentor.

Students often require substantial guidance in developing an achievable research question, understanding the literature, selecting appropriate methods, recognising practical constraints, organising data and interpreting evidence. This guidance is intended to develop scientific judgement rather than provide answers.

Importantly, guidance does not necessarily mean close day-to-day supervision. Most dissertation projects are designed as independent pieces of work. Supervisors are unlikely to accompany students during every field visit, laboratory session or analysis, or provide a detailed schedule for each day of the project. Instead, support often takes the form of scientific discussion, feedback on plans and analyses, recommendations for reading, examples of protocols and advice when challenges arise.

Many students underestimate the amount of self-organisation required. One of the first lessons of research is learning to manage time, make decisions and maintain progress without being given a detailed daily timetable. Scientific independence develops by working through uncertainty, reflecting on feedback and gradually becoming more confident in making informed decisions.

One of the first surprises for many dissertation students is that no one tells them exactly what to do every day. This is not a lack of supervision—it is one of the ways research teaches scientific independence.

Taught MSc dissertation

The supervisor remains an adviser and mentor, but greater independence is usually expected.

Students should increasingly be able to identify and evaluate relevant literature, justify methodological choices, prepare analyses or drafts for discussion, recognise weaknesses in their own reasoning and respond constructively to feedback.

Although supervisors may continue to provide substantial scientific advice, this does not imply continuous oversight of the project. Rather than directing the research day by day, supervisors increasingly expect students to arrive at meetings having made progress, reflected on challenges and identified specific questions for discussion.

Some MSc projects remain primarily individual educational projects, while others contribute directly to collaborative research programmes. Expectations regarding communication, documentation and project ownership should therefore be discussed explicitly at the outset.

MRes research

An MRes represents an important transition from being primarily a taught student to becoming an early-career researcher. The central aim remains education and training, but this training increasingly takes place through active participation in research rather than through taught modules alone.

An MRes researcher should be supported in developing the scientific knowledge, technical skills, critical thinking and professional judgement needed not only to complete the current project, but also to prepare for future careers in research, industry, government or environmental practice.

Many MRes projects are embedded within wider research programmes involving collaborators, shared datasets, field programmes, laboratory facilities or externally funded research. In these situations, supervisors often fulfil several roles simultaneously: mentor, scientific adviser, collaborator and principal investigator responsible for the wider research programme.

This creates opportunities that are not always available in more independent undergraduate projects. Students may gain access to established collaborations, specialist equipment, long-term datasets, advanced analytical methods and scientific networks. At the same time, collaborative research also brings additional responsibilities. Decisions, delays and changes to the project may affect colleagues, shared resources, field seasons, funding commitments or planned scientific outputs.

The purpose of these responsibilities is not to reduce academic freedom. Rather, they reflect the reality that research is increasingly conducted within collaborative scientific communities. Learning how to balance individual development with responsibility towards a wider research programme is itself an important part of becoming a professional researcher.

PhD research

A PhD represents a transition towards intellectual leadership and the development of an original and independent programme of research. Doctoral researchers continue to learn throughout their PhD, but they also become increasingly responsible for shaping scientific questions, making informed decisions and contributing new knowledge to their discipline.

Supervisors remain responsible for mentorship, constructive challenge, scientific guidance and, where appropriate, leadership of grants, research groups and collaborations. As the project develops, the supervisory relationship should increasingly resemble a scientific partnership in which ideas are discussed critically, assumptions are challenged openly and decisions are justified by evidence.

Many doctoral projects are closely connected to funded research programmes. These projects often involve significant investment in fieldwork, laboratory facilities, computing infrastructure, specialist equipment, technical support and collaboration with researchers across institutions or disciplines. Such investment provides exceptional opportunities for learning, but it also creates responsibilities for both supervisors and doctoral researchers.

Supervisors, particularly when acting as Principal Investigators, have responsibilities to universities, funders, collaborators and the wider research community to deliver high-quality research. Doctoral researchers contribute to these wider objectives while simultaneously developing their own scientific identity and expertise. These two aims should not be viewed as competing interests. When managed well, they reinforce one another: strong supervision supports both researcher development and the successful delivery of excellent science.

For most MRes and PhD projects, the ultimate goal extends beyond completion of the degree itself. Research should contribute meaningful, rigorous and reproducible knowledge that is capable of informing future work. In many disciplines, this means producing research of a quality suitable for peer-reviewed publication or other high-level scholarly outputs such as expert reports, datasets, software or methodological advances.

This does not mean that publication is guaranteed, nor that every project must produce a predetermined number of papers. Rather, researchers should recognise that successful research degrees usually require sustained focus on a coherent scientific question or closely connected programme of work. While research questions naturally evolve and interests often broaden during a project, frequent changes in direction can make it difficult to develop the depth of understanding and quality of output that advanced research degrees are intended to achieve.

Developing as a researcher therefore involves balancing curiosity with focus, scientific independence with collaboration, and personal learning with the shared responsibility to produce rigorous research that others can build upon.

TipBAHE Research Principle 1 — Scientific independence

Good supervision is not measured by how often a supervisor tells you what to do. It is measured by how effectively supervision helps you develop the judgement to decide what to do—and when to seek advice.

Scientific independence is not the absence of supervision. It is the gradual development of the judgement, confidence and responsibility needed to make increasingly well-informed scientific decisions.

How research expectations develop across degree levels

The precise expectations of each project depend on the programme, institution, discipline and research context. In broad terms, degree research develops from learning how research is conducted towards increasing responsibility for original and collaborative scientific work.

Degree stage Main purpose Supervisor’s role Researcher’s developing responsibility
BSc dissertation Learn how research is planned, conducted and communicated. Teacher, adviser and mentor. Organise an achievable project, engage with guidance and develop basic scientific judgement.
Taught MSc dissertation Conduct a substantial project with greater independence. Adviser, mentor and critical sounding board. Justify decisions, prepare work for discussion and manage the project more independently.
MRes research Develop as an early-career researcher through active participation in research. Mentor, scientific adviser, collaborator and sometimes Principal Investigator. Take substantial responsibility for planning, documentation, communication and agreed collaborative work.
PhD research Produce original knowledge while developing scientific independence and leadership. Mentor, critical scientific partner, collaborator and research leader. Lead an original programme of work, make defensible decisions and contribute reliably to wider scientific commitments.

The progression is not simply towards less supervision. Rather, it is towards a different form of supervision, in which scientific discussion, critical reflection and collaborative decision-making increasingly replace instructional guidance, while researchers take growing ownership of planning, communication and scientific judgement.

Planning the project

Good planning begins by connecting scientific ambition with realistic evidence.

Researchers should be able to explain:

  • the question the project addresses;
  • why the question matters;
  • what evidence is needed;
  • how that evidence can be obtained;
  • which decisions or dependencies may affect progress;
  • what outputs are expected; and
  • what can reasonably be completed within the available time and resources.

Plans should be treated as working scientific documents rather than fixed promises. Research often changes when new evidence, practical constraints or unexpected results emerge. Changes should be discussed and documented so that the project remains coherent.

Researchers should also identify any requirements for ethical approval, fieldwork permissions, health and safety procedures and risk assessment, data protection, biosecurity, animal welfare, access agreements or responsible handling of sensitive ecological information. Work requiring approval must not begin before the relevant permissions are in place.

Before the first project meeting

This section is particularly relevant to BSc and taught MSc students who are approaching a supervisor before the project has been fully defined. It can also help MRes and PhD researchers prepare for an early project-development meeting.

You do not need a complete proposal. You should, however, arrive with evidence of preparation.

Consider:

  • Which scientific questions or systems genuinely interest you?
  • Why might the topic matter?
  • What have you learned from initial reading?
  • What kind of evidence could address the question?
  • Which project approaches appeal to you?
  • What constraints might affect feasibility?
  • What specific advice or decision do you need from the meeting?

Reading a small number of relevant peer-reviewed papers usually makes the first discussion more productive. The purpose is not to master the literature before meeting, but to arrive ready for scientific discussion.

Communicating throughout the project

Effective communication is part of the research, not an administrative addition to it.

Communication should help clarify:

  • what has been completed;
  • what has been learned;
  • what remains uncertain;
  • which decisions are needed;
  • which problems or dependencies may affect progress;
  • what should happen next; and
  • who is responsible for each action.

Different projects can use different systems. Appropriate arrangements may include weekly or fortnightly meetings, short written updates, shared project logs, research notebooks, shared folders, version-controlled repositories, annotated figures, analysis reports and written notes recording decisions.

No single format is universally correct. The system should be proportionate, reliable and useful to the people whose work depends on the project.

TipBAHE Research Principle 2 — Communication

The purpose of communication is not to report activity. It is to improve scientific thinking and research quality.

Good communication explains what has been learned, what remains uncertain, what decisions are required and how the project should progress. Meetings, written updates and shared records are scientific tools that strengthen research rather than administrative obligations.

Reporting progress rather than activity

A useful update should not simply list tasks.

Instead of reporting:

I read papers and worked on the analysis.

a stronger update might explain:

I compared three approaches for modelling detection probability. The current model produces unstable estimates when sampling effort is low. I have summarised the diagnostics and need advice on whether to simplify the model or revise the data structure.

Progress communication should help answer:

  • What have we learned?
  • What evidence supports that conclusion?
  • What remains unresolved?
  • What have you already tried?
  • What decision or advice is now needed?
  • What is the next output or milestone?

Accountability and formal commitments

The level and form of accountability differ among projects.

For BSc and many taught MSc dissertations, the main formal obligations arise from programme requirements, assessment deadlines and agreed supervision arrangements. These degrees are educational programmes, and supervision should support the student in meeting the intended learning outcomes.

MRes and PhD researchers may additionally hold scholarships, studentships, contracts or project-linked funding. They may contribute to funded objectives, shared datasets, field schedules, laboratory workflows, reports or planned publications. These arrangements can create responsibilities beyond submission of the degree itself.

Researchers should therefore understand:

  • the formal conditions of their programme or funding;
  • agreed project milestones;
  • dependencies involving collaborators or facilities;
  • expectations for data management and documentation;
  • reporting requirements;
  • planned outputs; and
  • procedures for raising delays or difficulties.

General BAHE guidance does not replace degree regulations, scholarship terms, employment contracts, grant conditions or formal supervisory agreements. Where responsibilities are unclear, they should be discussed directly and recorded.

TipBAHE Research Principle 3 — Responsibility

Greater freedom in research is accompanied by greater responsibility—for the project, collaborators and the integrity of the science.

As researchers progress from BSc through MSc to MRes and PhD, they are expected to take increasing ownership of planning, communication and scientific decision-making. Where research contributes to collaborative programmes, funded projects or scholarships, this also brings responsibilities towards colleagues, funders and the wider research community.

Flexibility requires clarity

BAHE does not require every researcher to communicate in exactly the same way. A flexible system can be highly effective when expectations are clear.

For example:

  • one researcher may benefit from a weekly meeting;
  • another may work independently and provide a structured fortnightly update;
  • a computational project may use a continuously updated repository and analysis report;
  • a field project may require frequent coordination during sampling and less frequent meetings during later analysis; and
  • a collaborative project may require shared progress records even when individual meetings are less frequent.

Flexibility should not become uncertainty about whether progress is visible or whether agreed responsibilities are being met. Supervisors and researchers should review the communication system when it no longer supports the project.

Communication and supervision arrangements should recognise that researchers are people as well as scientists. Health, disability, caring responsibilities, part-time study, remote working and other personal circumstances can shape how individuals participate most effectively. Flexibility should therefore support equitable participation without weakening clear scientific, collaborative and programme expectations. Scientific excellence and human understanding are not competing values. BAHE aims to work with integrity by recognising different strengths, perspectives, circumstances and ways of thinking, while supporting each person to contribute at the highest level their capabilities allow. We are united by a shared commitment to rigorous science, but we achieve it by working together as individuals—not by expecting everyone to work in exactly the same way.

Raising problems early

Delays, failed methods and unexpected results are normal parts of research. Concealing them or waiting until deadlines are close usually reduces the available solutions.

Researchers should communicate early when:

  • data collection is delayed;
  • access to sites, samples or equipment changes;
  • methods are not working;
  • analysis reveals unexpected limitations;
  • project scope becomes unrealistic;
  • responsibilities are unclear;
  • collaboration is breaking down; or
  • personal circumstances are affecting progress.

Early communication is not evidence of failure. It is part of responsible project management and scientific judgement.

Advice should lead to development and output

Supervision involves a substantial investment of time, expertise, infrastructure and, in some projects, grant or scholarship resources.

The purpose of this investment is not only to provide an educational experience. It should support progressive development of both the researcher and the research.

Advice should therefore lead to documented scientific progress, such as:

  • a clearer research question;
  • an improved study design;
  • documented data;
  • reproducible analysis;
  • interpretable figures;
  • a stronger argument;
  • a thesis chapter;
  • a report;
  • a presentation;
  • a dataset or code resource; or
  • a publication or other agreed output.

Not every project will produce the same outputs, and not every result will be publishable. The expectation is not guaranteed success. It is sustained, rigorous work that converts learning and advice into the strongest defensible scientific contribution the project can achieve. Progress may include demonstrating that an approach is unsuitable, identifying a serious limitation, documenting a negative result or deciding not to pursue an unsupported interpretation. These can be valuable outcomes when the reasoning and evidence are rigorous and clearly recorded.

TipBAHE Research Principle 4 — Scientific progress

Research progress is measured by developing understanding and defensible scientific outputs, not simply by completing tasks.

The purpose of supervision is not merely to finish a dissertation or thesis. It is to transform discussion, feedback and experience into increasingly rigorous questions, stronger evidence, better analyses and scientific outputs that contribute meaningfully to knowledge.

Shared responsibility for rigorous science

Supervisors are responsible for providing appropriate guidance, constructive criticism and honest scientific advice. Researchers are responsible for preparing, communicating, documenting decisions and completing agreed work. Collaborators are responsible for contributing according to their agreed roles.

These responsibilities are connected. Strong supervision cannot replace researcher engagement, and researcher independence does not remove the need for critical discussion.

The shared objective is to produce work that is scientifically meaningful, methodologically defensible, transparent about uncertainty, appropriately documented, communicated with integrity and useful to others where possible.

A practical preparation framework

Before a supervision or project meeting, prepare five short elements:

  1. Progress: What has changed since the previous discussion?
  2. Evidence: What results, literature, figures or drafts support the update?
  3. Interpretation: What do you currently think the evidence means?
  4. Question: What decision, criticism or advice is needed?
  5. Next steps: What should be completed, by whom and by when?

For early-stage students, supervisors may help structure these elements. As researchers develop, they should increasingly lead this process.

When expectations or advice differ

Scientific disagreement is a normal part of research. Researchers should be able to question advice respectfully, explain alternative interpretations and ask for the reasoning behind important decisions.

When expectations, feedback or responsibilities are unclear:

  • summarise the issue in writing;
  • identify the specific decision or expectation that needs clarification;
  • discuss it directly at the next appropriate opportunity;
  • record any agreed changes; and
  • use formal programme or institutional support routes when concerns cannot be resolved within the supervisory relationship.

Seeking clarification is not disloyal or confrontational. Clear expectations protect the researcher, the supervisor and the quality of the project.

Data, authorship and shared research

Projects involving shared data, code, samples, equipment or collaborators should establish expectations early.

Researchers and supervisors should discuss:

  • where data and code will be stored;
  • who may access and reuse project materials;
  • how changes and analytical decisions will be documented;
  • how authorship and contributorship will be considered;
  • which outputs are expected during and after the degree; and
  • how unfinished collaborative work will be managed if circumstances change.

These discussions should be revisited as the project develops. Early clarification reduces misunderstandings and supports fair recognition of scientific contributions.

Reflecting on your research journey

There is no single correct way to organise a research project. Some researchers benefit from regular face-to-face meetings, others from written progress updates, shared project logs, research notebooks or collaborative working documents. The most effective approach depends on the nature of the research, the people involved and the stage of the project.

What matters is not whether your project looks like somebody else’s, but whether your approach is helping you make sustained scientific progress while supporting effective communication with supervisors and collaborators.

From time to time, take a step back and ask yourself:

  • Am I making steady progress towards meaningful scientific outputs?
  • Am I preparing thoughtfully for discussions with my supervisor?
  • Am I seeking advice when it is genuinely needed?
  • Am I documenting my work so that others could understand, evaluate or build upon it?
  • Are my current ways of working helping the quality and rigour of the science?
  • If I were supervising this project, would I clearly understand where it currently stands?

If several of these questions are difficult to answer confidently, it may be time to review how you are organising the project rather than simply working harder.

Research is rarely improved by struggling in isolation. A mentoring meeting is not an admission that something has gone wrong. Quite the opposite—it is often the most effective way to clarify priorities, identify obstacles and regain momentum before small problems become larger ones.

One of the first lessons of research is that independence does not mean solving every problem alone. It means recognising when scientific discussion will help you make better decisions.

Perhaps the simplest reflection is this:

If your project exists mainly in your head, it is probably time to talk to someone.

Ultimately, communication should never become a routine obligation. It should remain a scientific tool that helps transform ideas into rigorous research and meaningful outputs.

From student to scientist

Progression from BSc to MSc, MRes and PhD is not simply a progression towards less contact with supervisors. It is a progression towards greater scientific judgement, clearer ownership and more effective participation in collaborative research.

A developing scientist learns to ask increasingly meaningful questions, recognise the limits of evidence, make justified decisions, seek advice strategically, communicate uncertainty honestly, document work so others can understand it, contribute reliably to shared goals and transform training and discussion into rigorous scientific outputs.

The ultimate purpose of planning and communication is therefore not compliance with a meeting schedule. It is to create the conditions in which researchers, supervisors and collaborators can work together to produce excellent science.

Becoming part of a research community

Advanced research degrees are about more than completing an individual project. They also provide an opportunity to become an active member of a wider research community.

Researchers are therefore encouraged to engage regularly with seminars, workshops, journal clubs, research-group meetings and scientific discussions, both within and beyond their immediate project. These activities expose researchers to new ideas, methods and perspectives, help develop critical thinking and communication skills, and foster collaborations that often extend well beyond the degree itself.

Participation in the research community is rarely about attendance alone. Meaningful engagement—asking questions, discussing ideas, sharing experiences and contributing to scientific dialogue—is an important part of developing as a researcher.

While opportunities and expectations differ between programmes and projects, active participation in the intellectual life of a research community should be viewed as an integral part of research training rather than an optional extra.

TipBAHE Research Reflection — Research becomes valuable when it is shared

A dissertation or thesis is an important educational milestone, but it is not the final purpose of research. The wider purpose of scientific enquiry is to produce knowledge that others can evaluate, reproduce, challenge and build upon.

Scientific outputs—whether peer-reviewed publications, datasets, software, technical reports or other scholarly contributions—make research visible, transparent and useful to the wider community. They are not simply products to be completed, but evidence that ideas have been rigorously investigated, critically examined and communicated with integrity.

The degree recognises your development as a researcher. Scientific outputs ensure that your research becomes part of the collective development of scientific knowledge. Together, they represent both the journey of the researcher and the lasting contribution of the research.

An evolving guide

This guide reflects the current thinking within BAHE on research planning, supervision, communication and scientific development. It is intended to support researchers at different stages of their journey while recognising that no single framework can capture every project, discipline or supervisory relationship.

In keeping with the BAHE Research Philosophy, this guide should itself be viewed as an evolving knowledge object rather than a fixed set of rules. Scientific practice develops through critical discussion, new evidence, changing research cultures and the collective experience of researchers, supervisors and collaborators. Guidance on how we conduct research should be open to the same process of reflection and improvement.

Suggestions, constructive criticism and alternative perspectives are therefore welcomed. Future versions may incorporate new examples, different approaches to supervision, developments in research practice or insights from the wider academic community.

The principles presented here are intended to guide discussion rather than end it. Like research itself, they should remain open to review, refinement and debate.

Ultimately, the pursuit of excellent science depends not only on generating new knowledge, but also on continually improving the ways in which that knowledge is created, communicated and shared.
