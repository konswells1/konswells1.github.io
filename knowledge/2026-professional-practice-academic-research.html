Professional Practice in Academic Research

Professional Practice in Academic Research

Academic research depends not only on scientific expertise but also on how researchers collaborate, distribute responsibility, recognise contributions and exercise professional judgement across changing research relationships.

Professional practice in academic research: collaboration, responsibility and scientific judgement

Summary

Academic research is often communicated through its visible structures: research questions, methods, data, publications, grants and formal roles. In practice, however, science also develops through changing networks of people, ideas, expertise, resources and responsibilities. Researchers contribute different forms of intellectual and practical work, depend on one another in different ways, and enter collaborations with different levels of experience, authority, opportunity and career dependence.

Many expectations governing these relationships remain surprisingly implicit. Researchers are expected to contribute fairly, communicate professionally, respect intellectual contributions and recognise the work of others, yet what these expectations mean in practice is not always discussed. Terms such as collaboration, independence, ownership, leadership, trust and even contribution can therefore carry different meanings for different people.

This article introduces connected concepts for recognising and discussing these less-visible dimensions of academic research. Its purpose is not to prescribe a single model of collaboration, but to support the professional judgement needed to navigate research relationships with appropriate openness, clarity, accountability and scientific integrity.

Why this guide is needed: research is collaborative, but rarely symmetrical

Research is simultaneously an intellectual activity, a professional practice, a learning environment and a collaborative enterprise. Even apparently independent projects usually depend on ideas, infrastructure, funding, data, technical expertise, mentorship or previous work contributed by others.

These relationships are rarely symmetrical. They also operate within an academic environment that is both collaborative and competitive. Researchers may work closely towards shared scientific goals while simultaneously developing individual careers, competing for positions and funding, establishing intellectual independence and seeking recognition for their contributions. Institutions and research groups likewise collaborate while pursuing their own priorities and scientific visibility.

Neither collaboration nor competition is inherently problematic. Difficulties arise when collaboration is assumed to align everyone’s interests automatically, or when differences in authority, dependence, incentives and career consequences remain unrecognised.

A PhD researcher may know a dataset or experimental system better than anyone else while remaining dependent on supervisors for strategic guidance and career opportunities. A postdoctoral researcher may provide much of the intellectual and operational leadership of a project without controlling its funding or longer-term direction. A principal investigator may have considerable authority while depending heavily on the expertise and initiative of researchers within the group. Collaborators may contribute specialist knowledge, samples, data, infrastructure or intellectual perspectives without participating equally in every stage of a project.

Different roles therefore carry different combinations of contribution, responsibility, authority, dependence and professional interest. None of these can or should be inferred from seniority alone.

Learning to recognise and discuss these relationships should form part of mentorship and professional development. Doing so does not make collaboration more transactional; it allows researchers to work together with a clearer understanding of shared purposes, legitimate individual interests and the responsibilities accompanying different roles.

Shared and differing purposes come before shared work

Academic collaboration usually begins with something genuinely shared: an interest in science and in understanding a particular subject, problem or system. Curiosity, discovery and the ambition to produce rigorous and useful knowledge are important reasons why researchers choose to work together, and a common scientific purpose can provide a powerful basis for collaboration.

Shared scientific interest, however, does not mean that everyone enters a project with identical purposes. Nor should an appeal to science as a common endeavour make other legitimate objectives difficult to discuss. Degrees, publications, research training, career development, grant commitments and programme deliverables are not distractions from academic research; they are integral to how contemporary research is organised and sustained.

A PhD researcher may therefore be working simultaneously towards a degree, developing scientific independence and seeking publications that establish a research identity. A postdoctoral researcher may need to produce papers, demonstrate intellectual leadership and build a competitive profile for future fellowships or positions. A supervisor or Principal Investigator may be motivated by the same scientific questions while also being responsible for researcher development, high-quality outputs, commitments to funders and collaborators, and the continuity of a wider research programme.

These purposes frequently reinforce one another, but they are not interchangeable. Completing a thesis chapter, producing a publishable paper and meeting a funded project objective may involve much of the same research while carrying different priorities, standards and timelines. Friction can therefore arise not because collaborators lack commitment to the science, but because they have different assumptions about what the work should deliver, for whom and when.

Good professional practice involves making both shared purpose and important differences in purpose sufficiently visible. Useful questions include:

  • What scientific question or outcome brings us together?
  • What does successful participation in the project mean for each person?
  • Which outputs matter for a degree, contract, publication, fellowship, grant or wider collaboration?
  • Where do scientific, developmental and career objectives reinforce one another?
  • Where might priorities, responsibilities or timelines diverge?

Such clarification can strengthen rather than diminish scientific trust. It allows collaborators to recognise a genuine common commitment to the science while being transparent about the educational, professional and institutional purposes that accompany it.

Professional collaboration does not require everyone to want exactly the same thing from a project. It requires enough shared scientific purpose to make collaboration worthwhile, enough openness about differing purposes to make expectations realistic, and enough mutual understanding to coordinate expertise, responsibilities and ambitions as the work develops.

Contribution, responsibility, authority and recognition

Research relationships can be understood by asking four connected questions:

  • Contribution: What is each person contributing intellectually, practically, technically, financially or developmentally?
  • Responsibility: What has each person undertaken to deliver, support or oversee?
  • Authority: Who can make decisions, allocate resources, control access or substantially influence the direction of the work?
  • Recognition: How are contributions reflected in authorship, attribution, intellectual credit, opportunities or other forms of recognition?

These dimensions are connected but not interchangeable. Across them sits a further principle of reciprocity: are contributions, responsibilities, authority, opportunities and recognition reasonably aligned?

Reciprocity does not require equality. Research relationships are often necessarily asymmetric. A supervisor may contribute scientific direction, funding and infrastructure without undertaking day-to-day research; a postdoctoral researcher may provide specialist expertise and operational leadership while having limited formal authority; and a PhD researcher may develop substantial intellectual ownership of a project while continuing to receive supervision and support.

Such differences are not inherently problematic. What matters is whether they remain appropriate to the roles, circumstances and shared scientific purpose rather than becoming persistently one-sided. Seniority, control of resources or time invested alone does not establish intellectual ownership or entitlement to recognition.

Research can therefore be understood as involving reciprocal investment. Researchers contribute different combinations of time, expertise, intellectual effort, data, resources, mentoring, opportunities and professional trust. These contributions need not be equivalent or directly exchanged. Receiving supervision, resources or opportunities does not create unlimited obligations in return, but it normally carries reasonable responsibilities to prepare, contribute and progress agreed work. Conversely, providing funding, supervision or infrastructure does not establish ownership of every idea or contribution; contributing data does not automatically determine authorship; and working within a funded project does not preclude intellectual independence, initiative or substantive scientific contribution.

Professional practice therefore requires attention not simply to whether everyone contributes, but to how contribution, responsibility, authority and recognition develop in relation to one another. This matters particularly because publications, datasets, software, grants and presentations are simultaneously scientific products and records of contribution that can shape careers.

Trust is strongest when expectations are clear

Science depends heavily on trust. Researchers routinely share unpublished ideas, preliminary analyses, samples, code, data and opportunities before every eventual use can be specified.

Trust alone, however, cannot establish shared expectations.

Researchers may enter a collaboration with good intentions while holding different assumptions about who will analyse data, lead a manuscript, use material in future projects or receive authorship. Such differences may remain invisible until an important output approaches completion.

This can create an expectation gap: collaborators hold materially different assumptions about a relationship without recognising that those assumptions differ.

Publications provide an obvious example. One researcher may regard extensive data collection as the central contribution, while another places greater weight on developing the question, designing the study, obtaining resources, conducting analysis or writing the manuscript. Several perspectives may be reasonable. The problem arises when their implications for responsibility and recognition are never discussed.

Authorship policies and disciplinary guidance are important, but they cannot substitute for communication throughout a project.

The appropriate alternative to blind trust is therefore not distrust, but proportionate clarity.

Low-stakes collaborations between established colleagues may require little formal documentation. Multi-year projects involving career-defining datasets, several institutions, substantial resources or multiple anticipated outputs generally warrant greater clarity about responsibilities, access, leadership and recognition.

Written summaries, contribution statements, version control, data-management arrangements and early authorship discussions can support:

  • coordination — ensuring that collaborators understand decisions and next steps;
  • accountability — making important responsibilities visible; and
  • protection — providing a record when memories, circumstances or interests later diverge.

Researchers should not hesitate to seek reasonable clarification when responsibilities, timelines, access or anticipated contributions remain uncertain. Requesting clarity is part of professional research practice, not an indication of mistrust. Equally, accepting a substantive role carries responsibility to engage sufficiently in the communication and planning required to make that role operational. Research is often labour-intensive and interdependent; commitments that cannot be translated into workable responsibilities and timelines are difficult for others to plan around.

Expectations should also be revisited as research develops. People join or leave, analyses change, responsibilities shift and new outputs emerge. Such changes should prompt reconsideration of continuing responsibilities, access, contribution and recognition: leaving a project does not erase earlier contributions, just as earlier involvement does not establish entitlement to every subsequent output.

If expectations become contested, earlier trust or the absence of a detailed written agreement does not leave researchers without reference points. Institutional codes of research practice, disciplinary and journal authorship policies, and contributorship guidance such as CRediT can help evaluate contributions and responsibilities. Contributorship terminology does not itself determine authorship or author order, but can make different forms of contribution easier to discuss. Where substantial concerns remain, appropriate independent or institutional advice may be needed rather than relying on differences in seniority or negotiating power.

Documentation is therefore not inherently evidence of mistrust. Used proportionately, it can help sustain trust by making consequential expectations sufficiently clear for people to work together.

Academic incentives shape collaboration

Scientific collaboration takes place within systems of publication, funding, employment and career progression. These systems influence which outputs matter, when they matter and how strongly different researchers depend on them.

The same project can therefore carry different consequences across career stages. A publication may be one output among many for an established researcher while being critical to a PhD completion, fellowship application or postdoctoral career transition.

Recognising such differences does not require assuming selfish motives. Good professional practice makes consequential interests discussable rather than pretending that shared scientific purpose makes them disappear.

Visibility is a poor measure of contribution

Much of the work sustaining research is only partly visible. It may include developing ideas, securing funding, maintaining code or infrastructure, troubleshooting experiments, curating data, coordinating collaborations, training colleagues, reviewing drafts or creating opportunities for others.

Such less-visible work occurs at every career stage. Recognising it, however, does not mean that important contributions should remain unspecified. Where contributions affect responsibility, authorship or other recognition, making them sufficiently visible and describable is itself part of good professional practice.

Visible activity, time invested and seniority are therefore imperfect proxies for scientific contribution. What matters is the nature and significance of the contribution, together with the responsibilities undertaken.

Visibility also affects how researchers interpret one another’s circumstances. Collaborators may be unaware of competing responsibilities or constraints, while those with greater authority may underestimate the uncertainty, dependence or career consequences experienced by colleagues with less authority. These circumstances do not themselves determine contribution or recognition, but they can be important when interpreting expectations, timelines and responsibilities.

Making relevant contributions and constraints more visible cannot eliminate disagreement, but it provides a better basis for discussing workload, responsibility and recognition fairly.

Good science requires the ability to disagree

Scientific collaboration should not eliminate disagreement. Differences in expertise, experience, career stage, responsibility and scientific perspective mean that researchers may legitimately see the same project differently. A supervisor may emphasise a wider research programme while a PhD researcher focuses more immediately on degree requirements and developing independence. Collaborators may likewise differ in whether they seek the most direct answer to a question or use it to challenge existing assumptions, paradigms or approaches. Such differences can create friction, but they can also be an important source of scientific insight. Useful task disagreement concerns questions, evidence, methods and interpretation. It can strengthen research when people are able to question assumptions and evaluate alternatives. This differs from interpersonal conflict, where disagreement becomes focused on individuals or relationships rather than the scientific problem.

Research environments therefore benefit from psychological safety: a shared expectation that questions, uncertainty, mistakes and reasoned disagreement can be raised without inappropriate interpersonal or professional consequences.

Psychological safety does not mean avoiding criticism or lowering scientific standards. A rigorous research environment should make demanding scientific challenge possible while maintaining respect for the people involved.

This principle becomes particularly important where authority is unequal. Researchers should be able to question reasoning, identify uncertainty or raise concerns without scientific disagreement automatically being interpreted as disloyalty or inappropriate challenge.

When collaboration becomes difficult

Difficulties can arise for different reasons, and distinguishing among them can help identify an appropriate response. The following are useful distinctions rather than formal categories.

Scientific disagreement occurs when researchers reasonably interpret evidence, methods or priorities differently. Such disagreement is an ordinary and potentially productive part of science.

Misaligned expectations arise when collaborators assumed different responsibilities, timelines, rights or forms of recognition without making those assumptions sufficiently explicit.

Poor professional practice may involve repeated unreliability, avoidable delay, failure to communicate, inappropriate attribution or persistent failure to fulfil agreed responsibilities.

Potentially exploitative practice may arise where differences in authority or career dependence are used to obtain disproportionate advantage from another person’s work or to restrict appropriate recognition or opportunity.

Research misconduct has more specific institutional and regulatory meanings and should not be used casually to describe every disagreement or disappointing collaboration.

These distinctions help researchers respond proportionately rather than treating every problem as either harmless misunderstanding or deliberate misconduct.

A reasonable first response to uncertainty is often to clarify the issue: What was understood? What was undertaken? What contribution or decision is disputed? What outcome is needed?

Where appropriate, conclusions should be recorded and revisited. Persistent or serious concerns may require advice from mentors, research leaders or relevant institutional processes.

NoteApplying the concepts — recurring situations in research collaboration

Difficult research situations rarely reduce to simple questions of right and wrong. The concepts introduced above can help identify what requires clarification and which standards or responsibilities are relevant.

Authorship and corresponding authorship.
A supervisor expects to be corresponding author on a PhD researcher’s paper, while the researcher expects a leading role. Rather than treating seniority or convention as sufficient justification, clarify what corresponding authorship means in the relevant journal or discipline, who will assume its responsibilities, how intellectual leadership should be recognised, and whether shared corresponding authorship is appropriate.

Different contributions among co-authors.
A collaborator argues that co-authors should contribute equally. Contributions are rarely identical: conceptualisation, data collection, methods, analysis, supervision and writing may be distributed differently. The relevant questions are whether contributions meet applicable authorship criteria, are represented transparently and receive appropriate recognition.

Late, limited or changing contributions.
An anticipated contribution is repeatedly delayed, substantially reduced or changes as a project develops. Earlier expectations about authorship or responsibility need not determine the final outcome indefinitely. Actual contributions and continuing responsibilities should be revisited using applicable authorship guidance and contributorship statements where useful.

Ideas, proposals and intellectual continuity.
Research develops through sharing emerging ideas, but collaborators may differ greatly in their capacity to develop, fund or circulate them. When researchers discuss proposal outlines, hypotheses or developing approaches, reasonable clarity about confidentiality, intended use and attribution can therefore matter, particularly before ideas are shared beyond the original group. Neither openness nor seniority creates unrestricted permission to develop another person’s substantive contribution independently. Equally, ideas rarely have simple ownership, and excessive restriction can impede scientific exchange. Where intellectual contributions may become consequential, contemporaneous records and explicit discussion provide stronger reference points than assumptions alone. Data, code and access.
A researcher leaves a group while data, samples, code or analyses remain important to continuing work. Access and reuse should not depend solely on informal assumptions about who “owns” the project. Relevant agreements, data-management arrangements, intellectual contributions, funder or institutional requirements and continuing responsibilities should be considered explicitly.

Scope, workload and additional contributions.
A collaboration gradually requires substantially more analysis, writing, supervision or technical work than originally anticipated. Roles and recognition should be reconsidered when contributions change materially rather than assuming that an initial agreement covers unlimited additional work.

Shared mission and unequal interests.
A collaboration may be presented primarily in terms of an important scientific or societal mission while decisions about resources, outputs, opportunities or recognition remain unclear. Commitment to the science should support—not replace—transparent discussion of how work, resources, benefits and career opportunities are distributed.

Decision-making and informal circles.
Important decisions may sometimes be made by a smaller group because particular expertise, responsibility or confidentiality requires it. Problems can arise when consequential decisions repeatedly exclude researchers whose work or careers are directly affected. Appropriate participation depends on the decision, but relevant contributors should understand how important decisions are made and have reasonable opportunities to raise concerns.

Delayed outputs and competing responsibilities.
An early-career researcher has intellectual leadership of a paper but repeatedly postpones an output that also matters to collaborators or a wider research programme. Intellectual leadership carries responsibilities as well as recognition. Realistic timelines, barriers and responsibilities should be discussed and, where necessary, renegotiated rather than allowing either individual priority or group authority to determine progress indefinitely.

Across these situations, useful questions remain similar:

What are we trying to achieve? What was contributed? What responsibility was undertaken? Who holds relevant authority? How should this decision be made? What recognition is appropriate? What would reasonable reciprocity require?

Where expectations, decisions or contributions may later matter, document them proportionately and contemporaneously. Relevant records may include brief written summaries, contribution records, version histories, data-management arrangements, agreed timelines and substantive correspondence. Patterns of communication can also matter: repeated requests, responses, decisions—or prolonged absence of response—may help establish how responsibilities and expectations developed over time.

Documentation should be a natural part of good research practice rather than defensive record-keeping. Clear records of decisions, contributions, responsibilities and progress support planning, continuity, reproducibility and effective collaboration. The same records can also provide valuable reference points if expectations later diverge or a dispute arises.

Researchers, especially those earlier in their careers, should also feel able to seek perspective and advice when uncertain about professional practice. This should be done with appropriate discretion: concerns can often be discussed with trusted mentors, advisers or institutional contacts without unnecessarily disclosing confidential data, unpublished ideas or sensitive information about collaborators. Professional confidentiality should protect legitimate research interests, not prevent researchers from obtaining advice or discussing research practice responsibly.

If disagreement persists, institutional, disciplinary, journal or funder guidance can provide reference points beyond differences in seniority or negotiating power.

Professional judgement is more than knowing the rules

Codes of conduct, authorship criteria, employment policies and institutional procedures provide important boundaries. They cannot anticipate every research situation.

Professional practice therefore depends on scientific and professional judgement.

This includes knowing:

  • when informal discussion is sufficient and when an agreement should be recorded
  • when openness strengthens science and when confidentiality is appropriate
  • when to seek advice and when to exercise independence
  • when disagreement should be pursued and when compromise is reasonable
  • when an expectation is unclear rather than unfair
  • when changing circumstances require responsibilities to be renegotiated
  • when a pattern of behaviour warrants wider advice or intervention
  • how one’s own authority or dependence affects the situation.

Judgement develops through experience, but experience alone does not guarantee good judgement. Reflection, mentorship, transparent discussion and exposure to different research environments all matter.

The aim is neither to become suspicious of collaborators nor to assume that goodwill resolves every ambiguity. It is to develop the capacity to recognise what a situation requires.

From independence to stewardship

Early research training often emphasises becoming independent. Independence matters, but it is only part of professional development.

As careers develop, researchers usually become more deeply interconnected with others. They may supervise students, mentor colleagues, coordinate collaborations, control resources, review manuscripts, influence recruitment, lead grants or create opportunities affecting other careers.

Greater scientific independence therefore brings increasing stewardship.

Stewardship means recognising that authority over knowledge, resources and opportunities carries responsibility for how they are used. Good research leadership is not demonstrated simply by producing one’s own outputs, but by creating conditions in which rigorous science can be conducted, different expertise can be integrated, contributions can be recognised fairly and other researchers can develop.

This responsibility applies at different scales. A PhD researcher supervising an undergraduate already exercises a form of scientific stewardship. A postdoctoral researcher coordinating a collaborative analysis does so by establishing workable processes and recognising contributions. Principal investigators and senior researchers carry broader responsibilities because their decisions can influence resources, research direction and careers.

Professional development in academia is therefore not a simple journey from dependence to independence. It is a changing pattern of learning, contribution, interdependence, leadership and stewardship.

Working professionally in a dynamic research community

Academic research cannot be reduced to a fixed set of roles or rules. People, projects and collaborations change. Responsibilities evolve. New opportunities create new dependencies, and scientific questions connect researchers across groups, institutions and disciplines.

What remains important is the ability to make the relationships underlying research sufficiently visible to support good science.

Several principles follow:

  • Shared scientific purpose should be clear enough to make collaboration meaningful, while important differences in purpose should remain discussable.

  • Contribution should be visible enough to be recognised.

  • Responsibility should be clear enough to support accountability.

  • Authority and decision-making should be exercised proportionately and transparently.

  • Recognition should reflect substantive contribution rather than status alone.

  • Reciprocity does not require equality, but persistent one-sidedness warrants attention.

  • Trust should be supported by proportionate clarity rather than substituted for it.

  • Expectations should be revisited as projects, contributions and circumstances change.

  • Scientific disagreement should be possible without becoming interpersonal conflict.

  • Increasing independence should develop into responsible stewardship of research, people and opportunities.

These principles do not remove uncertainty from academic life. Nor should they replace disciplinary norms, institutional policies or professional codes of conduct.

Their purpose is to provide language for discussing aspects of research that too often remain unspoken.

TipBAHE Research Practice — Make professional practice discussable

Excellent research communities require more than goodwill. They need language through which researchers can discuss how they work together.

Concepts such as contribution, responsibility, authority, recognition, reciprocity, expectation gaps and proportionate clarity provide useful reference points. They are not fixed rules and should themselves remain open to critical scrutiny. Their value lies in making expectations that might otherwise remain implicit easier to identify, question and revise.

This matters across career stages. Researchers should be able to ask how contributions will be recognised, what responsibilities have been undertaken or why a decision is being made without reasonable questions themselves being interpreted as inappropriate challenge. Equally, supervisors and research leaders should be able to clarify expectations around preparation, reliability and contribution without these discussions becoming personal judgements.

A common vocabulary cannot remove differences in authority or prevent poor practice. It can provide a more transparent basis for discussing them. Where expectations are disputed, discussion can move from who has greater seniority? towards more useful questions: What are we trying to achieve? What was contributed? What responsibility was undertaken? Who holds relevant authority? What recognition is appropriate? What would fair reciprocity require?

Within BAHE, these concepts provide practical language for applying the values of shared responsibility, scientific integrity, fairness and researcher development described in the BAHE Research Philosophy. They should be questioned and refined in the same spirit as scientific ideas: by asking whether they help researchers work together more rigorously, fairly and effectively.

For more practical guidance on establishing expectations, communicating with supervisors and collaborators, and managing research as it develops, see Planning and Communicating a Degree Research Project.

Closing perspective

Many consequential lessons of academic research are learned outside formal methodological training. Researchers gradually learn how to contribute to collaborations, integrate different expertise, interpret expectations, negotiate independence, recognise competing interests, protect intellectual contributions, disagree constructively, exercise authority and support the work of others.

Making these aspects of professional practice more explicit should not make research more transactional. Shared terminology and proportionate clarity can instead support more open collaboration because fewer consequential expectations depend entirely on assumption.

Professional academic research is therefore neither founded on blind trust nor sustained by constant suspicion. It depends on relationships in which shared purpose, trust, clarity, responsibility and recognition develop together—and on researchers acquiring the judgement to know what those relationships require as science, careers and collaborations evolve.
