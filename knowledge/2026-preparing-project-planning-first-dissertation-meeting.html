Preparing for Project Planning and Your First Dissertation Meeting

Preparing for Project Planning and Your First Dissertation Meeting

Good dissertation projects begin long before data collection starts. This guide explains how to prepare for project planning, think like a scientist, communicate effectively with supervisors and choose a project that is both scientifically rewarding and achievable.

Why this guide

This guide is written primarily for BSc and taught MSc students who are beginning to plan a dissertation or other independent research project. It is intended to help you prepare for the early stages of project development and your first discussions with a potential supervisor. Although some of the principles may also be useful for MRes and PhD researchers beginning a new line of research, those degrees often involve different expectations, collaborative research environments and project-planning processes.

Many students assume that a dissertation project begins with the first meeting with a supervisor. In reality, the strongest projects usually begin earlier—with curiosity, reflection, background reading and careful thinking about the scientific questions that genuinely interest you.

You do not need a complete research proposal before meeting a supervisor. Equally, supervisors are best able to help when they understand something about your interests, ambitions, previous experience and the kinds of questions you hope to investigate. Even a modest amount of preparation can transform an introductory meeting from a general conversation into a productive scientific discussion.

The purpose of this guide is therefore not to help you produce a finished project plan before your first meeting. Instead, it aims to help you think like a scientist from the outset: identifying worthwhile questions, considering different approaches, reflecting on your own interests and ambitions, and arriving prepared to discuss ideas openly with your supervisor.

Within BAHE, project planning is viewed as the first step in becoming a researcher. Learning how to ask meaningful questions, evaluate ideas critically, discuss them constructively and refine them in response to feedback is every bit as important as collecting data later in the project. A successful dissertation begins not with a perfectly formed proposal, but with curiosity, thoughtful preparation and a willingness to develop ideas through scientific discussion.

The purpose of a dissertation

A dissertation is more than an assessment. It is your opportunity to undertake an independent piece of academic research.

Whether your project is experimental, observational, computational, literature-based or applied, every dissertation is a scientific enquiry. The methods may differ, but they all share the same purpose: to investigate a meaningful question through systematic evidence, critical thinking and honest communication.

A dissertation is also different from most other pieces of assessed work. It is often the first opportunity to plan, conduct and communicate a substantial project that reflects your own interests, decisions and scientific judgement. For many students, it becomes the piece of work that best represents their development during their degree and one they continue to discuss in postgraduate applications, job interviews and professional settings long after individual module marks have been forgotten.

Not every student intends to become a professional scientist, nor should they. Many graduates pursue careers in conservation, environmental consultancy, government, education, industry or many other sectors. Whatever your future path, the ability to ask good questions, evaluate evidence critically, solve problems systematically and communicate conclusions clearly remains valuable.

Every dissertation therefore has two closely connected purposes:

  • to investigate an interesting and worthwhile question through rigorous academic enquiry; and
  • to develop the knowledge, skills and judgement that will support your future career.

Within BAHE, we believe these two aims reinforce one another. The strongest dissertations are characterised not simply by their results, but by the quality of the scientific thinking behind them. They demonstrate careful planning, systematic enquiry, critical evaluation of evidence and honest communication, while helping students develop the habits of curiosity, rigour and scientific integrity that underpin both research excellence and professional practice.

Not every dissertation is expected to make a major scientific discovery, nor should it. Many valuable projects contribute by testing ideas in a new context, adding carefully collected observations to a larger body of evidence, evaluating existing knowledge, or demonstrating that a scientific question can be investigated rigorously and transparently. Learning how to design and complete such work to a high standard is itself an important achievement. It prepares students to contribute confidently to research groups, professional practice and, for those who continue into research degrees, increasingly ambitious scientific enquiry.

From curiosity to a dissertation project

One of the greatest challenges when beginning a dissertation is often not having too few ideas, but having too many.

Biology, ecology and environmental science offer almost unlimited possibilities. You may be fascinated by particular organisms, ecosystems, diseases, environmental problems, conservation challenges, analytical methods or emerging technologies. Faced with so many potential directions, it is entirely normal to feel uncertain about where to begin.

Scientists face exactly the same challenge. They rarely start with a perfectly defined research question. Instead, they gradually transform broad interests into focused research projects through exploration, selective reading, critical reflection and discussion. Developing a project is therefore not a single decision but a process of progressively narrowing many interesting possibilities into one question that is both scientifically worthwhile and practically achievable.

A typical pathway might look like this:

Curiosity and interests
        ↓
Scientific theme
        ↓
Model or study system
        ↓
Selective reading and exploration
        ↓
Research question
        ↓
Evidence and methods
        ↓
Study design
        ↓
Dissertation project

This pathway is rarely linear. Reading may refine the research question, practical constraints may suggest a different study system, or discussions with your supervisor may identify more suitable methods. Developing a project is an iterative process in which ideas are continually tested, refined and sometimes replaced by stronger alternatives.

As you explore possible projects, ask yourself:

  • Which topics genuinely sustain my curiosity?
  • What do we already know, and where are the important uncertainties?
  • Which questions could make a worthwhile scientific contribution?
  • What evidence would be needed to answer those questions?
  • What kinds of fieldwork, laboratory work, computational analysis or desk-based research would the project involve?
  • Would I genuinely enjoy carrying out this work over several months?
  • Can the project realistically be completed with the available time, resources and expertise?

Selective background reading is one of the most valuable ways to support this process. Reading a small number of well-chosen papers does more than introduce the scientific background. It allows you to understand what different research projects actually involve, how researchers approach similar questions and whether the methods, working style and scientific challenges genuinely appeal to you.

These reflections are not simply about choosing a topic. They are about discovering the kind of research you would enjoy doing and identifying opportunities that best match your interests, ambitions and preferred ways of working.

For most BSc and taught MSc students, it is entirely normal to arrive at the first project meeting near the beginning of this journey. You do not need a finished proposal or a perfectly formulated research question. Instead, aim to arrive with genuine curiosity, evidence that you have begun exploring the literature and an open mind about how your ideas may develop.

Your supervisor’s role is not to choose your project for you, but to help you refine your ideas, identify worthwhile scientific questions, recognise practical constraints and develop a project that is both intellectually rewarding and achievable.

One of the first lessons of research is that choosing a project is itself part of scientific enquiry. It combines curiosity with evidence, imagination with critical thinking, enthusiasm with realism, and personal interests with opportunities to make a meaningful scientific contribution.

Exploring your scientific interests

Every research project begins with a broad area of interest, but successful projects rarely remain broad for long.

Scientists often develop long-term interests in particular organisms, ecosystems, diseases, environmental challenges or biological processes. Within these broad themes, however, they become increasingly interested in specific questions, unexpected observations or unresolved problems that deserve closer investigation.

As you begin thinking about a dissertation, ask yourself questions such as:

  • Which areas of biology or environmental science genuinely capture my attention?
  • Which topics do I find myself reading about beyond lectures?
  • Which scientific questions or environmental problems would I like to understand better?
  • Which lectures, practicals or field experiences have stayed with me, and why?

At this stage, you do not need to identify a precise research question. Instead, try to identify the broader scientific themes that genuinely motivate your curiosity.

The next step is to begin exploring those themes through selective reading and discussion. As you learn more, broad interests gradually become more focused. You begin to recognise which questions are already well understood, where important uncertainties remain, and which aspects are both scientifically worthwhile and realistic to investigate within a dissertation.

A successful dissertation therefore rarely begins with a title. It begins with curiosity about a broader area of science and gradually develops into a focused, answerable question through reading, reflection and scientific discussion.

From favourite species to model systems

Many students begin with a favourite species or group of organisms. That is not only normal, it is often how scientists begin as well. Genuine enthusiasm for a particular organism, habitat or ecosystem can provide lasting motivation for a research project and, for many researchers, even an entire career.

The next step, however, is to ask what that organism can help us understand. In science, organisms are often studied as model systems: they provide a way of investigating broader biological, ecological or environmental questions.

For example, rather than asking simply:

“Can I study butterflies?”

consider questions such as:

  • How does mowing intensity influence butterfly diversity in grasslands?
  • How do urban green spaces affect pollinator communities?
  • How do changes in habitat connectivity influence butterfly movement?

Here, butterflies are not the final objective of the research - they are the model system through which wider questions about biodiversity, habitat management and ecological processes can be investigated.

Similarly, an interest in tropical birds might develop into questions about island biogeography, migration, habitat fragmentation, disease ecology or predator-prey interactions. The birds remain central to the project, but they become part of a broader scientific enquiry.

Thinking in this way helps transform an interest into a research project. It connects personal curiosity with questions that are scientifically meaningful, feasible to investigate and capable of contributing to wider biological understanding.

You do not need to abandon the organisms or systems that first inspired your interest in biology. Instead, ask yourself:

What larger scientific question could this organism or system help me answer?

That question often marks the transition from simply choosing a study organism to developing a well-justified dissertation project.

Think about the skills and experience you want to develop

A dissertation is not only an opportunity to investigate a scientific question. It is also an opportunity to develop methods, judgement and professional experience that may remain valuable long after the project has finished.

As you compare possible projects, ask yourself:

  • Would I enjoy field ecology, laboratory work or desk-based research?
  • Would I like to develop skills in statistical modelling, GIS, molecular methods, remote sensing, programming, literature synthesis or data visualisation?
  • Which methods would strengthen future job or postgraduate applications?
  • Which project would give me useful examples of my abilities to discuss in an interview?
  • Which experience would best support the professional direction I hope to pursue?

Many graduates later discover that the methods, habits and confidence developed during a dissertation became more valuable than the particular organism or study system.

It is natural to be attracted to projects that sound exciting. However, an appealing title does not necessarily lead to an enjoyable or successful research experience. Spending several months struggling with methods you dislike is usually much less exciting than the project initially sounded.

Think not only about what interests you today, but also about which project will help you develop scientific thinking, practical skills and experience that continue to benefit you after the dissertation has been submitted.

A dissertation may last for a semester or a year; the skills, habits and confidence you develop through it may shape your career for much longer.

Independent projects and research-group projects

Dissertations can begin in different ways.

Some students develop their own ideas into an independent project, while others join an existing research programme within a research group, laboratory or long-term field study. Both approaches can provide excellent scientific training, but they often involve different forms of planning, communication and supervision.

Developing an independent project usually provides greater freedom to shape the research question, study design and direction of the work. Projects embedded within existing research programmes may provide access to specialist expertise, equipment, established datasets, long-term monitoring programmes, collaborative networks and scientific questions that would be difficult to investigate independently within the timescale of a dissertation.

Joining an existing research programme does not usually require less preparation. In many cases it requires more. Supervisors and collaborators need to understand your interests, motivation, previous experience and the skills you hope to develop in order to determine how a dissertation can be integrated successfully within the wider project.

Early discussions may therefore involve more than identifying a research question. They may also include conversations about how your work fits within the broader scientific objectives, which parts of the project will form your own dissertation, how data and resources will be shared, how communication will take place, and how collaborative working and open research practices will be managed throughout the project.

Projects embedded within research groups are often more selective, not because they are reserved for particular students, but because successful collaboration depends on preparation, mutual trust and a shared commitment to high-quality science. Demonstrating curiosity, initiative and thoughtful preparation helps supervisors judge how a project can best support both your development and the wider research programme. Within BAHE, the various sections of this website provide a useful starting point for understanding the group’s research interests, scientific approaches and collaborative culture, helping you decide whether a particular project or research environment aligns with your own interests, ambitions and ways of working.

Whatever route you choose, your dissertation should remain your own piece of academic work, demonstrating your understanding, scientific judgement and ability to conduct rigorous research. At the same time, learning how to contribute responsibly within a collaborative scientific environment is itself an important part of becoming a researcher.

Preparing for your first meeting

Your supervisor does not expect a finished dissertation proposal.

What they do hope to see is that you have begun thinking about your interests, explored some background literature and reflected on the kinds of research you would enjoy undertaking.

This preparation is not about impressing your supervisor. It is about providing enough information for a productive scientific discussion.

The better your supervisor understands your interests, current thinking and uncertainties, the better they can help you identify suitable opportunities, refine ideas and develop a project that is both scientifically rewarding and achievable.

For projects with limited places or those embedded within collaborative research programmes, thoughtful preparation also helps ensure that projects and supervisory time are allocated fairly, transparently and in ways that best support both student development and the wider research programme.

Preparing for your first project discussion

Before your first meeting, ask yourself whether you can answer most of the following questions.

1. Scientific interests

  • Which areas of biology or environmental science interest me most?
  • Why do these topics interest me?
  • Which scientific questions or problems am I most curious about?
  • Are there particular organisms, habitats or systems that particularly motivate me?

2. Developing a research question

  • Which organisms, study systems or processes could help investigate these questions?
  • What evidence would I need to answer them?
  • Could the project be approached through fieldwork, laboratory work, computational analysis, existing datasets, literature synthesis or a combination of methods?
  • Which aspects of the question seem scientifically interesting and achievable?

3. Understanding the practical work

Try to imagine yourself carrying out the research.

  • What kinds of data would need to be collected?
  • Which practical methods would be required?
  • Would the project involve field sampling, laboratory techniques, microscopy, molecular methods, GIS, statistical modelling, programming, remote sensing or other specialist approaches?
  • Which of these skills do I already have?
  • Which would I be interested in learning?

4. Feasibility

  • What equipment, software, facilities or datasets might be required?
  • Would the project depend on laboratory space, specialist equipment or field sites?
  • Would ethical approval, permits or access permissions be needed?
  • Are there seasonal constraints or time limitations?
  • Does the project appear realistic within the available dissertation period?

5. Background preparation

  • Have I read two or three relevant scientific papers?
  • What have I learned from them?
  • Which methods did the researchers use?
  • Which ideas or approaches particularly interested me?
  • Could I imagine carrying out similar research?

6. Skills and future development

  • Which scientific skills would I most like to develop?
  • Which analytical approaches would strengthen my future career?
  • Which project would provide experience that I would be proud to discuss in a future interview or postgraduate application?

7. Discussion with my supervisor

  • Which ideas would I most like to explore?
  • Which uncertainties would I value discussing?
  • Which practical constraints have I already identified?
  • What questions would I like to ask?

You do not need complete answers to every question. Rather, the checklist provides a useful starting point for a constructive scientific discussion and helps ensure that both you and your supervisor can make the most of your first meeting.

TipBAHE Project Planning Principle — Help your supervisor help you

The purpose of preparation is not to arrive with a finished project.

It is to arrive ready for a meaningful scientific conversation.

Supervisors can provide the best advice when they understand your interests, preparation, ambitions and uncertainties. Good project planning therefore begins not with the perfect idea, but with thoughtful preparation, open discussion and a willingness to refine your thinking.

Within BAHE, common guidance such as this checklist is intended to support fair, transparent and inclusive supervision. Shared expectations help students prepare effectively, enable supervisors to provide consistent and appropriate advice, and create a common framework through which both can reflect on whether the support and planning process is working well. Excellence in research is best achieved when openness, integrity and mutual respect guide every scientific discussion.

The first meeting is the beginning

A successful dissertation is rarely the result of a single brilliant idea.

It develops through repeated cycles of reading, discussion, critical reflection and refinement.

Your first meeting should therefore be viewed as the beginning of a scientific partnership rather than the moment when a complete project is decided.

Within BAHE, guidance such as this is intended to provide a shared framework for planning research and communicating expectations. It does not replace individual supervisory styles or the flexibility required by different projects. Rather, it aims to foster fair, transparent and supportive interactions in which both students and supervisors can work towards the same goal: developing rigorous science while supporting the growth of the researcher.

Shared principles also encourage reflection. They help students understand what good supervision should look like and help supervisors communicate advice consistently and transparently, while recognising that every researcher, project and pathway is different.
