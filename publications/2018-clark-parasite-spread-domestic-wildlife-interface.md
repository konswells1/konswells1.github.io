summary: >
  This study compiled the first global database of cat flea (Ctenocephalides
  felis) and dog flea (C. canis) infestations in wild mammals and combined it
  with host trait data to investigate the drivers of parasite spillover at the
  domestic animal–wildlife interface. Bayesian hierarchical phylogenetic
  models showed that anthropogenic habitat use, host phylogeny and body mass
  strongly influenced infestation risk. Cat fleas were found to be highly
  host-generalist, whereas dog fleas exhibited much greater phylogenetic host
  specificity. The study highlights the importance of invasive reservoir hosts
  and human-modified habitats in facilitating flea transmission to wildlife.

knowledge_summary: >
  The study provides one of the first global macroecological analyses of flea
  spillover between domestic animals and wildlife. By integrating systematic
  host–parasite records with phylogenetic comparative modelling and
  functional-phylogenetic host specificity analyses, it demonstrates that host
  evolutionary history and ecological traits jointly determine parasite
  infestation risk. The findings distinguish two contrasting spillover
  strategies: the broadly host-generalist cat flea, capable of ecological
  fitting across diverse mammalian lineages, and the more phylogenetically
  constrained dog flea. The work advances understanding of parasite host
  switching, invasive reservoir hosts and disease emergence at the
  human–domestic animal–wildlife interface within a One Health framework.

impact_statement: >
  Human modification of landscapes and the spread of invasive mammals create
  new opportunities for parasite spillover between domestic animals and
  wildlife. Reducing contact with invasive reservoir hosts is therefore an
  important strategy for limiting flea infestations and the transmission of
  flea-borne pathogens across natural and anthropogenic ecosystems.

plain_language_summary: >
  Cats and dogs can carry fleas that also infest many wild mammals. By
  combining information from studies around the world, this research showed
  that cat fleas can infest an exceptionally wide variety of wildlife species,
  while dog fleas are restricted to a smaller group of closely related hosts.
  Wildlife living in or near human-modified environments is more likely to be
  exposed to cat fleas, particularly where invasive animals such as feral
  cats, dogs, foxes and rats are present. These findings show how urbanisation
  and invasive species can increase parasite transmission between domestic
  animals and wildlife, with implications for biodiversity, animal health and
  One Health management.
