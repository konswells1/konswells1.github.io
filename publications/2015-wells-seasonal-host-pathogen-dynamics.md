# Abstract

# Purpose: Original published abstract.
abstract_original: |-
  Infectious diseases can exert a strong influence on the dynamics of host populations, but it remains unclear why such disease-mediated control only occurs under particular environmental conditions. We used 16 years of detailed field data on invasive European rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) in Australia, linked to individual-based stochastic models and Bayesian approximations, to test whether (i) mortality associated with rabbit haemorrhagic disease (RHD) is driven primarily by seasonal matches/mismatches between demographic rates and epidemiological dynamics and (ii) delayed infection (arising from insusceptibility and maternal antibodies in juveniles) are important factors in determining disease severity and local population persistence of rabbits. We found that both the timing of reproduction and exposure to viruses drove recurrent seasonal epidemics of RHD. Protection conferred by insusceptibility and maternal antibodies controlled seasonal disease outbreaks by delaying infection; this could have also allowed escape from disease. The persistence of local populations was a stochastic outcome of recovery rates from both RHD and myxomatosis. If susceptibility to RHD is delayed, myxomatosis will have a pronounced effect on population extirpation when the two viruses coexist. This has important implications for wildlife management, because it is likely that such seasonal interplay and disease dynamics has a strong effect on long-term population viability for many species.

  This study combined 16 years of rabbit monitoring data with stochastic
  individual-based demographic and epidemiological models, approximate Bayesian
  computation and sensitivity analysis to investigate seasonal interactions
  among reproduction, rabbit haemorrhagic disease and myxomatosis.

# Knowledge-network summary.
knowledge_summary: >
  The publication shows that disease severity emerges from phenological
  matching between host recruitment and pathogen activity rather than from
  pathogen parameters alone. Juvenile insusceptibility and maternal antibodies
  delay exposure to rabbit haemorrhagic disease, while recovery from
  myxomatosis alters the pool of hosts available to the second virus. These
  coupled seasonal processes determine recurrent epidemics and stochastic
  local-population persistence.

# Scientific or societal significance.
impact_statement: >
  Effective biological control and wildlife-disease forecasting require models
  that integrate seasonal host demography, age-dependent immunity and
  interactions among co-circulating pathogens.

# Non-technical summary.
plain_language_summary: >-
  Rabbit diseases are most severe when the timing of breeding and virus
  activity creates many susceptible animals at the same time. Young rabbits
  can be temporarily protected by natural resistance and antibodies from their
  mothers, delaying infection. Simulations and long-term field records showed
  that interactions between rabbit haemorrhagic disease and myxomatosis can
  strongly affect outbreak severity and whether local rabbit populations
  persist.
