abstract_original: |-
  Global change in the Anthropocene has modified the environment of almost any species on earth, be it through climate change, habitat modifications, pollution, human intervention in the form of mass drug administration (MDA), or vaccination. This can have far-reaching consequences on all organisational levels of life, including eco-physiological stress at the cell and organism level, individual fitness and behaviour, population viability, species interactions and biodiversity. Host-parasite interactions often require highly adapted strategies by the parasite to survive and reproduce within the host environment and ensure efficient transmission among hosts. Yet, our understanding of the system-level outcomes of the intricate interplay of within host survival and among host parasite spread is in its infancy. We shed light on how global change affects host-parasite interactions at different organisational levels and address challenges and opportunities to work towards better-informed management of parasite control. We argue that global change affects host-parasite interactions in wildlife inhabiting natural environments rather differently than in humans and invasive species that benefit from anthropogenic environments as habitat and more deliberate rather than erratic exposure to therapeutic drugs and other control efforts.

summary: >
  This conceptual review examines how global change modifies host–parasite
  interactions across biological scales and challenges conventional parasite
  management. It contrasts the dynamics of humans and domestic animals with
  those of wildlife, emphasising differences in population connectivity,
  habitat change, exposure history and access to control interventions. The
  review argues that parasite control must account for eco-epidemiological
  dynamics, environmental heterogeneity, multi-host interactions and temporal
  variation rather than relying on static prevalence or host-range information.

knowledge_summary: >
  The publication develops a systems-level framework for parasite management
  under global change. It links within-host infection processes, among-host
  transmission, host population structure, environmental change, invasive
  species and control interventions within a unified eco-epidemiological
  perspective. It identifies two recurrent analytical pitfalls: targeting
  control solely according to observed prevalence and projecting parasite
  interactions across complete host ranges without considering environmental
  suitability and interaction plasticity.

impact_statement: >
  Effective parasite control under global change requires dynamic,
  multi-species and spatially explicit decision frameworks that anticipate
  ecological feedbacks and unintended consequences across human, domestic
  animal and wildlife systems.

plain_language_summary: >
  Climate change, land-use change, pollution, urbanisation and medical
  interventions can all affect how parasites spread. This review explains why
  parasite control cannot be based only on current infection levels or maps of
  where host species occur. Better decisions require understanding how hosts,
  parasites, environments and control measures interact over time and across
  landscapes.
