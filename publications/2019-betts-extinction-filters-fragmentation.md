# Abstract

# Purpose: Original published abstract.
abstract_original: |-
  Habitat loss is the primary driver of biodiversity decline worldwide, but the effects of fragmentation (i.e., the spatial arrangement of remaining habitat) are debated. We tested the hypothesis that forest fragmentation sensitivity—affected by avoidance of habitat edges—should be driven by historical exposure, and therefore species’ evolutionary responses, to disturbance. Using a database containing 73 datasets worldwide (4,489 animal species), we found that the proportion of fragmentation-sensitive species was nearly three times higher in regions with low versus high rates of historical disturbance (i.e., fires, glaciation, hurricanes, and deforestation). These disturbances coincide with a latitudinal gradient in which sensitivity increases six-fold at low versus high latitudes. We conclude that conservation efforts to limit edges created by fragmentation will be most important in the world’s tropical forests.

summary: >
  This global comparative study analysed 73 forest-fragmentation datasets
  containing 4,489 animal species to test whether historical disturbance
  regimes explain geographical variation in sensitivity to forest edges.
  Fragmentation-sensitive species were substantially more prevalent in
  historically low-disturbance regions, especially at low latitudes.

# Knowledge-network summary.
knowledge_summary: >
  The study provides a global test of the extinction-filter hypothesis by
  linking contemporary animal responses to forest edges with long-term
  exposure to fire, glaciation, tropical storms and historical forest loss.
  Communities in frequently disturbed regions contained fewer forest-core
  and edge-avoiding species, consistent with past local extinction or
  evolutionary adaptation filtering out sensitive taxa. This historical
  contingency helps reconcile divergent conclusions about fragmentation
  effects across regions and taxa.

# Scientific or societal significance.
impact_statement: >
  Forest fragmentation does not affect all regions equally: historically
  low-disturbance tropical forests contain a greater proportion of
  edge-sensitive species and therefore warrant particularly strong protection
  from new forest edges and landscape fragmentation.

# Non-technical summary.  
plain_language_summary: >-
  Animals do not respond to forest fragmentation in the same way everywhere.
  By combining information on 4,489 species from around the world, the study
  found that animals living in regions with little historical disturbance are
  much more likely to avoid forest edges. Species in frequently disturbed
  regions may already have adapted to disturbance, or sensitive species may
  have disappeared. Preventing new forest edges is therefore especially
  important in tropical forests.
