# Concise publication summary.
summary: >
  This review examines the challenges of modelling infectious disease
  emergence at the wildlife–human interface, emphasizing the dynamic
  ecological processes governing pathogen spillover, host shifting and
  zoonotic transmission. It synthesizes current knowledge on spillover
  pathways, environmental change, ecosystem dynamics and modelling
  approaches, and identifies priorities for developing predictive
  eco-epidemiological models that account for uncertainty, multiple host
  species and anthropogenic drivers of disease emergence. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0} :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

# Knowledge-network summary.
knowledge_summary: >
  The paper advances a systems perspective in which zoonotic emergence is
  viewed as an emergent property of dynamic wildlife–livestock–human
  interfaces shaped by ecological interactions, environmental change and
  pathogen diversity. It argues that accurate prediction of spillover
  requires integrating surveillance, ecological theory, community ecology,
  host–pathogen networks, environmental drivers and mechanistic epidemic
  models across multiple spatial and temporal scales, while explicitly
  representing uncertainty arising from incomplete observations of
  spillover processes. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2} :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3} :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

# Scientific or societal significance.
impact_statement: >
  The review provides a conceptual roadmap for developing next-generation
  predictive models of zoonotic disease emergence by integrating ecological,
  epidemiological and environmental processes. It highlights that improved
  surveillance, richer ecological data and ecosystem-based modelling are
  essential for anticipating and mitigating future pandemics driven by
  climate change, land-use change and other anthropogenic pressures. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5} :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

# Non-technical summary.
plain_language_summary: >
  Many new human diseases begin in wildlife, but predicting when they will
  spread to people is extremely difficult because transmission depends on
  changing interactions among animals, humans and the environment. This
  review explains why better disease prediction requires combining
  information about wildlife, pathogens, climate, land use, human behaviour
  and ecosystem dynamics into integrated models that can identify where and
  when spillover is most likely to occur and help reduce future pandemic
  risk. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7} :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}
