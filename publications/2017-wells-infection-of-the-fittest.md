# Summaries
# =======

summary: >
  This study developed a Bayesian state-space mark–recapture model that
  integrated survival, reproduction, tumour growth and disease progression
  to investigate the demographic consequences of devil facial tumour disease
  (DFTD) in a wild Tasmanian devil population over ten years. Contrary to
  expectations, individuals that eventually became infected exhibited higher
  pre-infection survival and reproductive success than individuals that never
  developed tumours. Although increasing tumour burden strongly reduced
  survival, DFTD disproportionately removed individuals with the highest
  reproductive value, providing new insights into the demographic and
  evolutionary consequences of emerging wildlife diseases.

knowledge_summary: >
  The study demonstrates that infection risk is not necessarily greatest among
  the weakest individuals in a population. Instead, DFTD selectively affects
  high-fitness, socially dominant Tasmanian devils that contribute
  disproportionately to population growth. By integrating epidemiological and
  demographic processes within a unified Bayesian state-space framework, the
  study links individual disease progression to population-level dynamics and
  provides a general methodological framework for investigating emerging
  wildlife diseases. The findings reshape understanding of disease-mediated
  selection by showing that pathogens can remove the individuals with the
  greatest reproductive value, thereby altering both population viability and
  evolutionary trajectories.

impact_statement: >
  Devil facial tumour disease has its greatest demographic impact because it
  disproportionately infects and ultimately removes the most reproductively
  successful individuals in the population. The study introduces an analytical
  framework that can be applied broadly to quantify the demographic and
  evolutionary impacts of emerging infectious diseases in wildlife.

plain_language_summary: >
  Most diseases are expected to affect the weakest individuals in a population,
  but this study found the opposite for Tasmanian devils. Devils that became
  infected with the contagious facial tumour generally survived longer before
  infection and produced more offspring than animals that never developed the
  disease. Once tumours became large, however, survival declined rapidly. By
  combining ten years of field observations with advanced statistical
  modelling, the researchers showed that this cancer removes the animals that
  contribute most to future generations, making its effects on wild
  populations much greater than would be expected from mortality alone.
