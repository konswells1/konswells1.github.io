abstract_original: |-
  Biodiversity offsetting aims to compensate for ecological losses from development through conservation and restoration. However, its long-term outcomes under repeated impact–offset cycles remain uncertain. Using a conceptual forest succession model, we evaluated how offset outcomes are affected by interactions among compensation scale, duration of protection, habitat conversion rate, and land availability constraints. Additionally, we considered that these outcomes may differ across biodiversity components, including habitat maturity associated with successional habitat-forming species and metapopulation dynamics of persistent colonizers and successional specialists. We showed that effective offsetting requires restoring areas larger than those impacted and maintaining protection over timescales aligned with ecological recovery, particularly where ongoing habitat conversion reshapes landscape structure. High rates of habitat conversion consistently reduced offsetting success, particularly for colonizing species reliant on habitat quality and long-term protection. Our results further showed that delayed ecological responses and cumulative impacts can undermine offsetting efforts that appear effective in the short term. Robust offset design must therefore explicitly account for temporal lags, dynamic landscape trajectories, adequate spatial compensation, and multiple biodiversity indicators to avoid the ecological fallacy that short-term restoration success equates to long-term biodiversity and ecosystem integrity.

image_caption: >-
  Conceptual framework for optimizing biodiversity offsetting under habitat succession and colonization dynamics. The framework illustrates the iterative cycle linking habitat impacts, biodiversity offset implementation, ecological succession and species recolonization. Habitat conversion is compensated through offset actions that restore habitat-forming vegetation, generating dynamic landscapes composed of impacted, restored and protected patches. As restored habitats mature, colonization depends on species dispersal abilities and habitat age requirements, producing long-term changes in biodiversity across successive impact–offset cycles. The accompanying simulation outputs demonstrate how alternative offsetting strategies and management decisions—including compensation ratios, conservation commitment, restoration duration, habitat protection and landscape connectivity—can influence biodiversity trajectories over time, providing a quantitative basis for designing more effective biodiversity offset policies.

summary: >
  This study develops a spatially explicit conceptual simulation framework to
  evaluate biodiversity offsetting under repeated habitat loss and ecological
  restoration. By integrating forest succession, habitat conversion, landscape
  dynamics and metapopulation processes, the analyses show that successful
  biodiversity offsetting generally requires compensation areas larger than
  impacted habitats, long-term conservation commitments aligned with ecological
  recovery, and explicit consideration of ecological time lags. High habitat
  conversion rates consistently reduced biodiversity outcomes, demonstrating
  that static offset ratios are insufficient for achieving long-term no-net-loss
  objectives.

knowledge_summary: >
  The publication advances biodiversity offset science by integrating habitat
  succession, species colonization dynamics and repeated development–offset
  cycles within a single ecological modelling framework. It demonstrates that
  offset effectiveness emerges from interactions among spatial compensation,
  conservation duration, landscape dynamics and species-specific ecological
  responses rather than from fixed compensation ratios alone. The study
  identifies ecological recovery time, habitat conversion rate and long-term
  protection as fundamental determinants of successful biodiversity offsetting
  and provides a transferable framework for evaluating conservation policy under
  dynamic landscape change.

  offset design.

  and account for the time ecosystems need to recover. The results also show
