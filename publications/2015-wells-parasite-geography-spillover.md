# Purpose: Original published abstract.
abstract_original: |-
  Aim Geographic spread and range expansion of species into novel environments may merge originally separated species assemblages, yet the possible drivers of geographic heterogeneity in host–parasite associations remain poorly understood. Here, we examine global patterns in the parasite assemblages of two rat species and explore the role of parasite acquisition from local pools of host species.
  
  Location Global.
  
  Methods We compiled a global data set of helminth parasites (n = 241 species) from two rat species (Rattus rattus species complex, R. norvegicus) and, concomitantly, from all other mammal species known to be infected by the same parasites. We used an inverse Bayesian modelling approach to explicitly link species-level to community-level infestation probabilities at different geographic scales and alleviate the shortcoming of sampling bias.
  
  Results Patterns of species richness and turnover of parasites in the two focal rat species revealed clear biogeographic structure with lowest species richness and most distinct assemblages in Madagascar and highest species richness and least distinct assemblages in the Palaearctic region. Parasite species richness and turnover across regions were correlated for the two focal hosts, although they were associated with distinct assemblages within regions. Infection probability of a focal host with any given parasite was clearly related to infection probability of the local species pool of wildlife hosts with that same parasite. Infection probability of other mammal species infected with these parasite species, in turn, decreased with their taxonomic distance to the genus Rattus.
  
  Main conclusions Our study demonstrates the importance of spillover of parasites from local wildlife hosts to invasive rats on global patterns of host–parasite associations. Considering both changes in local pools of host species and the global distributions of parasite and pathogen diversity in consistent model frameworks may therefore advance the forecasting of species-level infestation patterns and the possible risk of disease emergence from local to global scale.

summary: >
  This global analysis compiled 12,405 host–parasite records for 241 helminth
  species associated with black rats, Norway rats and other mammals, then used
  an inverse hierarchical Bayesian model to estimate geographic patterns and
  spillover from local host communities.

# Knowledge-network summary.
knowledge_summary: >
  The study links parasite occurrence in invasive rats to both regional
  parasite geography and the composition of local mammal host assemblages.
  Parasite richness and turnover varied strongly among zoogeographic regions,
  while infection probabilities in rats closely tracked the prevalence of the
  same parasites in local wildlife hosts. The modelling framework also
  addressed incomplete sampling and false absences, allowing species-level and
  community-level information to be combined.

# Scientific or societal significance.
impact_statement: >
  Global patterns of infection in invasive hosts cannot be understood from host
  range expansion alone; they depend strongly on parasite biogeography and
  spillover from locally available wildlife reservoirs.

# Non-technical summary.
plain_language_summary: >-
  Invasive rats do not carry the same parasites everywhere. By combining global
  parasite records with a Bayesian model, the study found that rats are more
  likely to acquire parasites that are already common in local mammal
  communities. Parasite communities therefore reflect both broad geographic
  history and spillover from wildlife in the regions that rats invade.
