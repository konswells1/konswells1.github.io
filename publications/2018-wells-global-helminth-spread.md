# Abstract
# =====

# Purpose: Original published abstract.
abstract_original: |-
  CChanges in species distributions open novel parasite transmission routes at the human–wildlife interface, yet the strength of biotic and biogeographical factors that prevent or facilitate parasite host shifting are not well understood. We investigated global patterns of helminth parasite (Nematoda, Cestoda, Trematoda) sharing between mammalian wildlife species and domestic mammal hosts (including humans) using >24,000 unique country-level records of host–parasite associations. We used hierarchical modelling and species trait data to determine possible drivers of the level of parasite sharing between wildlife species and either humans or domestic animal hosts. We found the diet of wildlife species to be a strong predictor of levels of helminth parasite sharing with humans and domestic animals, followed by a moderate effect of zoogeographical region and minor effects of species’ habitat and climatic niches. Combining model predictions with the distribution and ecological profile data of wildlife species, we projected global risk maps that uncovered strikingly similar patterns of wildlife parasite sharing across geographical areas for the different domestic host species (including humans). These similarities are largely explained by the fact that widespread parasites are commonly recorded infecting several domestic species. If the dietary profile and position in the trophic chain of a wildlife species largely drives its level of helminth parasite sharing with humans/domestic animals, future range shifts of host species that result in novel trophic interactions may likely increase parasite host shifting and have important ramifications for human and animal health.

# Concise publication summary.
summary: >-
  This global empirical study analysed 24,486 country-level
  host–parasite records covering 4,507 helminth species and 1,366 mammal
  hosts. Hierarchical Bayesian models linked helminth sharing between
  wildlife and seven focal human or domestic hosts to mammalian ecological
  traits, phylogeny, zoogeographical region and sampling effort, and the
  resulting models were used to project global association-risk patterns.

# Knowledge-network summary.
knowledge_summary: >-
  The study identifies trophic ecology as a major organising mechanism of
  helminth sharing at the human–domestic animal–wildlife interface. Wildlife
  diet class consistently outweighed most climatic, habitat and life-history
  predictors, while geographical projections showed correlated risk patterns
  across humans, dogs, cats, livestock and commensal rats. The results connect
  host traits, multispecies trophic interactions, biogeography and parasite
  host shifting within a global One Health framework, while emphasising that
  uneven sampling and presence-only records constrain precise local prediction.

# Scientific or societal significance.
impact_statement: >-
  The findings provide a macroecological basis for anticipating how wildlife
  range shifts, animal trade, biological invasions and altered food-web
  interactions could change helminth transmission pathways relevant to human,
  domestic-animal and wildlife health.

# Non-technical summary.
plain_language_summary: >-
  Humans, domestic animals and wild mammals can carry many of the same
  parasitic worms. Using more than 24,000 records from around the world, this
  study found that what wild mammals eat was more informative about parasite
  sharing than most of the other ecological traits examined. Similar
  geographical patterns occurred for parasites associated with people, pets,
  livestock and rats. Changes in species distributions and food-web
  interactions could therefore open new routes for parasites to move among
  wildlife, domestic animals and humans.
