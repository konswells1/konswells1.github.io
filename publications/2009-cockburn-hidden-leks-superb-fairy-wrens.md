# =====
# Abstract
# =====

# Purpose: Original published abstract.
abstract_original: |-
  Female superb fairy-wrens Malurus cyaneus initiate extragroup fertilizations by forays to the territory of preferred males just before sunrise, two to four days before egg laying. Over a prolonged breeding season, males advertise their availability to foraying females by singing during the dawn chorus. The study shows that males commence dawn advertisement at the same time of year regardless of quality or status; subordinate males advertise near dominant males or use dominant song perches despite punishment; low-quality dominants and helpers sing from territory boundaries near attractive neighbouring dominants; spatial clusters share a common trill-song dialect implicated in extragroup choice; and some paternity leaks to lower-quality helpers and neighbours through satellite behaviour. These findings extend the hidden-lek hypothesis to birds defending year-round all-purpose territories and suggest that parasitic satellite behaviour can contribute to polyandry.

# Knowledge-network summary.
knowledge_summary: >
  The study extends hidden-lek theory to a cooperatively breeding bird with
  year-round all-purpose territories. Males clustered their dawn-song posts,
  shared local dialects and competed intensely for access to females making
  predawn fertilization forays. Subordinates and neighbours gained paternity by
  acting as satellites around attractive early-moulting males.

# Scientific or societal significance.
impact_statement: >
  The findings show that sexual selection can reorganise social and spatial
  behaviour even in territorial cooperative breeders, and that satellite
  tactics can help explain widespread multiple mating in birds.

# Non-technical summary.
plain_language_summary: >-
  Before sunrise, male superb fairy-wrens gather their song posts into small
  clusters and use shared local song dialects to attract females seeking
  matings outside their social group. Lower-quality males position themselves
  near attractive males and sometimes gain paternity despite being attacked,
  creating a hidden lek within a territorial bird population.
